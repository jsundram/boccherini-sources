Music Encoding
Field Guide

Eight formats ordered by what they can hold, an honest matrix of what survives conversion between them, and the engines that draw them. The short version: almost nothing is truly lossless — and the axis that predicts where converters break is not richness but whether a format states things outright or infers them from context.

The richness axis

Ordered from least to most expressive. But "richness" is really four independent dimensions, and a format can sit high on one and at zero on another — which is why a single ladder misleads if you read only the order.

Read the dimensions, not just the rung. **kern is as semantically explicit as MEI yet deliberately holds almost no layout — it exists for analysis, not printing. MIDI sits at the bottom for notation while carrying performance detail nothing above it records. "Richer" is only meaningful relative to the dimension you need.

Each cell records what is actually known about that conversion — measured here, reported in the literature, or true by construction — rather than what the formats claim to support.

The strongest published result is a negative one. Nápoles López, Vigliensoni and Fujinaga converted four 100-file corpora across Humdrum, LilyPond, MEI and MusicXML and found that discrepancies appeared in every translation path tested. In the worst case close to 30% of notes landed at a different offset and about 5% lost articulations and ornaments. Several files could not be parsed at all after conversion.

They also name the asymmetry directly: "translating symbolic music format A to B can potentially lose more information than translating B to A." Fidelity is a property of a direction, not of a pair.

Nápoles López, Vigliensoni & Fujinaga, “The effects of translation between symbolic music formats,” Music Encoding Conference 2019 — PDF

Eight working rules

What the matrix above implies for building pipelines.

  1. One canonical internal model; convert only at the edges.

    N converters, not N². Every format becomes a reader or a writer against one in-memory representation you control.

  2. Choose the canonical format by your hardest consumer.

    Scholarly apparatus and provenance → MEI. Moving between notation apps → MusicXML. Computational analysis → **kern. Not by whichever is easiest to emit.

  3. Keep the source. Derived formats are caches.

    If you can re-derive everything from the original encoding, a converter bug is an afternoon. If you have migrated and discarded, it is an archaeology project.

  4. Verify with a reader you did not write.

    Writing both halves means validating code against its own assumptions — a closed loop that can be confidently wrong. An independent reader is worth more than any test you can author.

  5. Round-trip tests beat unit tests for format work.

    They surface the cases you would never have thought to write: sticky state that leaks across a chord, a group delimiter that silently swallows the rest of a bar.

  6. Lossiness is directional — make the downhill direction loud.

    Terser→richer is safe elaboration. Richer→terser needs an explicit policy that raises rather than truncates. Silent degradation is the failure mode that costs years.

  7. Survey before you build — and check the API, not the docs.

    Verovio does write Plaine & Easie (renderToPAE); a dir() would have shown it in one call. Roughly 40% of the converter written for this page duplicated renderToPAE, xml2abc and musicxml2ly. Head to head, the existing tools matched the hand-written ones exactly.

  8. MIDI is not a notation format.

    It has no enharmonic spelling, no layout, no editorial layer. It is performance data. Using it as an interchange format is the most common mistake in this space.
